Context

Background

With two accessibility UX specialists supporting an enterprise product organization, we had solid upstream processes — accessibility reviews, annotations, and handoffs to developers. But we had a blind spot: we didn't know what was happening after development. Were annotations being followed? Were bugs coming from design decisions or engineering implementation? Were they even being categorized correctly?

The A11y UX product lifecycle had clear steps at the start, but the back half was full of unknowns.

The gap

We had good processes at the start of the lifecycle but no visibility into what happened after handoff. Without data, we couldn't tell if our upstream work was actually reducing bugs downstream.

Process Design

Defect Refinement

I started by establishing a weekly defect refinement process. The goal was simple: get the team aligned on what was coming out of accessibility testing, where bugs were originating, and whether we agreed on solutions.

Where were bugs coming from?

Were accessibility issues originating in design decisions or engineering implementation?

Were bugs categorized correctly?

Were Jira tickets mapped to the right WCAG Success Criteria?

Did we agree on solutions?

Were the design and engineering teams aligned on how to fix each issue?

What we learned from refinement

The weekly reviews surfaced three immediate benefits. First, we aligned as a team on defect templates, how to categorize bugs, and what kinds of solutions we should push for — creating consistency that hadn't existed before.

Second, reviewing bugs every week gave us a live window into how our products were performing. We could see, week by week, whether our upstream accessibility work was making an impact.

"Reviewing bugs every week gave insight into how our products were performing, so we could see if our contribution was making an impact."

Third — a useful lesson in process design — we discovered the synchronous weekly meeting wasn't the right format with offshore teams. We moved to an asynchronous review model, which worked better for everyone and freed up meeting time.

Data Strategy

Diving Deeper Into the Data

After a few weeks of refinement reviews, I noticed we were sitting on richer data than we were using. The bugs themselves were telling a story — but only if we captured the right fields.

I added root cause tracking to Jira tickets. This single change gave me a way to identify systemic gaps in accessibility practice and track progress in closing them over time.

The insight

Root cause data turned a list of bugs into a feedback loop. When a spike appeared, I could immediately identify whether it came from annotations not being followed, annotations not being included, or missing/incorrect information — and respond with a targeted intervention.

Three root causes, three responses

1
Annotations were not followed

Response: followed up with designers and developers, implemented handoff calls, led targeted annotation trainings.

2
Annotations were not included

Response: followed up with designers, improved communication around fast follows, included annotations as a requirement in UX tickets.

3
Annotations had missing or incorrect information

Response: treated each instance as a learning opportunity, updated the annotation kit to close the gap.

System Design

Closing the Loop

With defect refinement and root cause tracking in place, the A11y UX lifecycle finally had a complete feedback loop. The two unknown stages became Implementation Support — a new touchpoint where accessibility specialists help developers during build — and a weekly bug list that connected testing outcomes back to upstream practice.

Results

Impact

The results were immediate. After implementing the defect refinement process, I saw a sharp decrease in bugs. The data made it easy to identify and address the cause of any spikes — and to demonstrate the value of the process over time.

The average number of bugs per month was 3.4. Removing December and January — which had explainable spikes — brought that average below one bug per month.

What's Next

Future Goals

This process is working — and there's a clear path to making it work even harder.

More consistent data over time

Continued data collection will build a more complete picture of the process's long-term impact on reducing accessibility defects.

Expand to engineering bugs

The current process only covers design-origin bugs. A parallel track for engineering will capture the full picture.

Roll out across Verily

Right now this is Enterprise-only. Adopting the process org-wide will create significantly more impact at scale.
