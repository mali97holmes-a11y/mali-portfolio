Background

The Problem

Feeding yourself sounds simple. For people with executive dysfunction, chronic illness, or fluctuating energy, it isn't. Deciding what to make, checking what you have, building a grocery list, actually going shopping — each step is a decision, and decisions have a cost.

Plenty of meal prep apps exist. None of them are designed for the days when you have very little to give. They assume ideal conditions: a stocked pantry, time to cook, the bandwidth to make choices. Food Friends starts from the opposite assumption.

The gap

Existing meal prep apps don't adapt to disability needs or fluctuating energy levels. Food Friends is designed specifically for those days — and built by someone who lives them.

Concept

The Opportunity

I started Food Friends as a project for an AI building cohort — a chance to actually build something I wanted to exist in the world. The original vision was ambitious: a meal planning assistant, a pantry scanner, a grocery list generator, a habit-building system to help people develop sustainable routines around food.

The core idea: an app that asks how much energy you have today — not how much you wish you had — and surfaces recipes and support that match that level. Some days that means a real cooked meal. Some days it means a microwave quesadilla, and that needed to be a first-class option, not an afterthought.

"Some days it means a microwave quesadilla — and that needs to be a real option, not an afterthought."

Product Thinking

Scoping the MVP

Very quickly I realized that building everything at once wasn't going to work. In order to make the core experience feel right, I had to let go of features I cared about. This was one of the most valuable parts of the project — it gave me a real, hands-on perspective on product management and the discipline of prioritization.

The biggest pain point I wanted to solve first: deciding what to make, and then getting the groceries. The core flow became a short survey — how much energy do you have, how much time, any dietary needs — that outputs recipe suggestions and a grocery list. Pantry scanning, grocery app integration, and habit tracking went to the backlog.

Visual Design

Design System

Accessibility was the starting point for every visual decision. I built the design system in Claude — prioritizing calming colors that still met WCAG contrast requirements. A soft periwinkle purple, warm cream, and sage green form the core palette, with enough contrast to be legible without being harsh or visually overwhelming.

The goal was a visual experience that felt gentle and unhurried — right for an app that's often used on low-energy days when everything feels like too much.

Technical Decisions

Tech Stack

I chose SwiftUI for two reasons: I wanted the app to run natively on iPhone so I could use it myself and share it with friends who would benefit, and I wanted accessibility to meet a high bar. SwiftUI's native integration with VoiceOver, Dynamic Type, and system focus management means the foundations are solid without custom implementations for every component.

The AI layer uses the Anthropic API with careful prompt engineering to guide recipe generation toward genuinely disability-friendly outputs. Before pushing to TestFlight, I also audited the codebase to make sure no API keys were exposed client-side — a detail that's easy to overlook when building fast.

Why native SwiftUI

SwiftUI gives you VoiceOver, Dynamic Type, and system accessibility behaviors for free. For an app built around disability inclusion, accessibility shouldn't be retrofitted — it should be foundational.

Design Iteration

Testing & Iteration

Once the core flow was running, I started testing it — and immediately hit a problem. Even the recipes tagged as "easy" weren't actually easy enough for someone running on empty. They still required chopping, multiple dishes, or sustained time at the stove. That's not low-energy. That's just normal cooking.

I rewrote the AI prompt's energy level rules from scratch. The "running on empty" tier now has strict constraints: no cutting, no chopping, no more than 3–4 steps, nothing requiring more than 2 minutes at the stove. Microwave quesadillas became the reference point — if a recipe is harder than that, it doesn't belong in this tier.

"If it's harder than a microwave quesadilla, it doesn't belong in the running-on-empty tier."

This kind of disability-informed prompt engineering is where AI tools require the most careful human judgment. The model doesn't know what executive dysfunction feels like. That's the designer's job — and it's not something you can outsource.

Product Design

Expanding the Information Architecture

Once the first flow was working well, I stepped back to think about the bigger picture. A key insight from designing for executive dysfunction: fewer tabs means fewer decisions every time you open the app. A five-tab structure would create decision fatigue before you'd even started.

I landed on a home screen where users set their energy for the day, then pick from three focused tasks: make a quick meal, plan a grocery list, or meal prep. Starting with intent — rather than dumping the user into a feature menu — means less back-and-forth mid-flow and a much lower cognitive burden.

Reflection

What This Taught Me

Food Friends was built almost entirely with AI assistance — and that's exactly why it's been such a useful learning experience. AI is remarkably good at getting ideas out of your head and into something functional, fast. But it doesn't replace the thinking.

The most important moments in this project were the ones where I slowed down: realizing the "easy" recipes weren't easy enough for someone with limited energy, deciding to cut half the planned features to protect the core experience, choosing a tab structure based on cognitive load rather than feature completeness. None of that came from the AI — that came from design thinking.

"AI helps you build faster. Design thinking helps you build the right thing."

Working with AI as a collaborator also sharpened my ability to give precise, thoughtful direction. Prompting well is a design skill — you have to understand what you want, why you want it, and how to communicate constraints clearly. That maps directly onto writing briefs, giving feedback, and defining requirements.

The project is still in progress, and that's intentional. There's still a pantry integration to build, an energy adaptation feature to design, and plenty more iteration to do. But the core is working — and using it myself has already been the validation I needed that this is worth finishing.
