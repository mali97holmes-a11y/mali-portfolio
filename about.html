About Me

I make the web work
for everyone.

Hi, I'm Mali — an accessibility and UX designer making digital products more inclusive, more usable, and honestly just better. I've been doing this work since 2020 and I genuinely love it, which probably comes through in how much I talk about it.

Bay Area based Open to work

How I got here

I didn't set out to become an accessibility specialist — I stumbled into it and immediately knew it was exactly what I was supposed to be doing. There's something uniquely satisfying about work that sits at the intersection of design craft and genuine impact on people's lives.

Since 2020, I've had the chance to do that work at scale — leading accessibility programs at Verily and CVS Health, building annotation kits and decision trees from scratch, designing inclusive patient experiences, and using data to make the case that accessibility isn't a nice-to-have.

These days I'm expanding into broader UX design, because the skills transfer beautifully: deep empathy for users, a system-level view of how products work, and an instinct for where things break down.

How I work

My design philosophy starts with the people who are most often left out of the room when decisions get made. If I design for someone with a screen reader, a motor disability, or executive dysfunction, I almost always end up with something that works better for everyone.

I'm collaborative to the core — I do some of my best thinking in conversation, and I genuinely enjoy working with engineers, product managers, and content designers. I don't believe in throwing specs over a wall. Handoffs are the beginning of a conversation, not the end of one.

I also believe in leaving things better than I found them — whether that's a Figma file, a design process, or a team's understanding of accessibility.

Building community

Accessibility work can feel pretty isolating, especially when you're one of a small handful of specialists in a large organization. In 2021, I was invited to co-host A11y Bay — a Bay Area meetup for accessibility practitioners — and it ended up being the thing that kicked off my accessibility career in a real way. Being around other people who care about this work as much as I do changed everything.

Beyond the meetup, I mentor people who want to break into accessibility, and I speak across bootcamps, universities, and master's programs to help the next generation of designers understand why inclusive design matters — and how to actually do it.

Community building is a design problem, just with people instead of pixels. You have to listen carefully, reduce friction, and make sure everyone in the room feels like they belong there.
